# Ethics

Use this method when a decision may misallocate benefits, burdens, voice, consent, responsibility, or harm.

## Method

1. Identify every materially affected party, including absent and future parties
2. Map who creates value, who receives it, who bears risk, and who pays when the plan fails
3. Test whether consent is informed, voluntary, and supported by meaningful alternatives
4. Identify duties, rights, promises, and relationships that should not be reduced to preferences
5. Check distribution, reversibility, precedent, and whether a less powerful party has effective voice
6. Test for extraction. Ask whether the plan depends on lock-in, hidden information, coercion, transferred costs, or a continuing supply of disadvantaged counterparties
7. State the strongest ethical case for the plan and the strongest objection
8. Repair each valid failure where possible. State plainly when repair would destroy the plan's mechanism

Healthy self-interest can create value for both sides. Service can become unsustainable self-sacrifice. Predation extracts value through asymmetry or impaired consent. Do not collapse these into a simple selfish versus altruistic scale.

## Optional virtue check

Use this only when the user asks for a virtue-oriented analysis or when character expressed by the action is itself decision-relevant. Examine the one or two virtues doing the most work:

- honesty and integrity
- courage
- prudence
- justice
- temperance
- humility
- diligence
- responsibility
- beneficence

For each, check both deficiency and excess. Do not score all nine by default. Pair every failure with a repair.

## Completion criterion

Finish when affected parties, consent, value and risk flows, duties, the strongest objection, and any possible repair are explicit. Do not declare an action ethical merely because it is legal or mutually profitable.
