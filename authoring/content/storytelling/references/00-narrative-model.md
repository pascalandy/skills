# Shared narrative model

## Movement

> A story is an experience organized through time in which something matters,
> something exerts pressure, and something changes.

Movement may be external, internal, relational, informational, symbolic, collective,
atmospheric, or perceptual.

## Four distinct layers

- **Material** consists of the facts, memories, images, scenes, sounds, phrases,
  sources, and constraints the author has
- **Story** consists of the events, relationships, intentions, causes, pressures, and
  changes the audience can reconstruct
- **Telling** controls selection, order, duration, point of view, distance, omission,
  repetition, and revelation
- **Expression** realizes the work through language, image, sound, body, silence,
  rhythm, space, layout, interaction, and medium

Do not repair one layer automatically by rewriting another.

## Three contracts

- The **truth contract** determines whether the work is fictional, factual,
  autobiographical, documentary, source-based, or hybrid
- The **audience promise** names what the audience is offered, such as understanding,
  feeling, discovery, witness, reconsideration, participation, decision, or action
- The **intervention contract** sets the role of the skill

Use the least invasive role that can satisfy the assignment:

- **Mirror** reflects and clarifies
- **Coach** unlocks progress through targeted questions
- **Architect** shapes trajectory and form
- **Editor** diagnoses and corrects minimally
- **Coauthor** explores and drafts with the author
- **Writer** produces a complete version from supplied material

## Movement map

Examine these elements without making each one mandatory:

- **Attention carrier**: person, group, place, object, system, or idea
- **Starting state**: equilibrium, habit, lack, expectation, workflow, or question
- **What matters**: value, relationship, desire, need, risk, or curiosity
- **Progressive force**: pressure, resistance, discovery, contrast, or accumulation
- **Agency**: choice, tactic, refusal, adaptation, observation, or collective action
- **Price**: cost, risk, delay, tradeoff, contradiction, or loss
- **Turn**: action, discovery, recognition, reversal, or rereading
- **Arrival state**: changed situation, relationship, understanding, capability, or
  ambiguity
- **Residue**: question, image, emotion, debt, limit, or possibility that remains

A story need not depend on central conflict. Other engines include inquiry,
accumulation, contrast, relationship, ritual, transformed repetition, humor,
anticipation, drift, atmosphere, testimony, system evolution, audience participation,
a problem becoming legible, or a design decision producing consequences.

## Coherence and audience state

Chronology alone does not create movement. Between units, look for a meaningful
relation such as cause, consequence, motivation, condition, contrast, echo,
repetition, escalation, revelation, recontextualization, simultaneity, or deliberate
association.

Deliberate fragmentation remains coherent when it creates a recognizable reading
logic.

For the whole work and for each unit, determine:

- what the audience knows, believes, feels, or anticipates on entry
- what changes on exit
- what mechanism creates the shift
- why the shift belongs here

## Mechanism

Movement becomes actionable through a mechanism: a craft choice provides a cue, the
audience performs an operation, and that operation changes its state. The relation is
causal and conditional, not a label applied after the fact.

The operating branches point to `references/05-narrative-mechanisms.md` when they need
to select, diagnose, or transfer a mechanism. Do not load the map as a checklist.

## Tellability and theme

**Tellability** answers:

> Why this story, from this teller, to this audience, now?

It may arise from surprise, injustice, recognition, contradiction, a revealing detail,
a rare experience, a shared question, a singular voice, a difficult truth, or a useful
explanation of how change became possible.

Treat theme as an active question. Let it emerge through choices, costs, repetitions,
contrasts, consequences, silences, tradeoffs, and what the work refuses to resolve.
Do not decide on a moral that every unit must prove.

## Claim ledger

Use the claim ledger for consequential claims in factual, source-based, or hybrid
work. Keep two axes distinct.

The **basis** records where a claim comes from:

- **Stated**: a person or authoritative source explicitly asserts it
- **Observed**: it is directly visible in supplied material, an artifact, or behavior
- **Reconstructed**: several elements support a marked reconstruction
- **None**: no available source establishes it

The **status** records what the material permits:

- **Supported**: the basis is adequate for the claim as worded
- **Inferred**: the interpretation is plausible but not explicit
- **Unknown**: available material does not answer the question
- **Contradicted**: established elements disagree
- **Option**: a creative possibility, not a factual claim

Record the source, date, and version when they affect meaning. A statement proves that
someone made the statement, not that its content is independently true.

Never turn stated material, observation, reconstruction, inference, or an option into a
stronger claim without new support.

## Decision ledger

Keep creative and editorial decisions separate from factual claims. For durable work,
record each decision with one status:

- **Proposed**: available for consideration
- **Selected**: currently governs the work
- **Rejected**: considered and not chosen
- **Superseded**: replaced by a later selected decision

Record the rationale, owner when relevant, and date. A selected decision is not a
supported factual claim.
