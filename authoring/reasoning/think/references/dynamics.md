# Dynamics

Use this method when outcomes depend on how actors respond to rules, incentives, information, power, and one another.

This is a general strategic method. It is not an application of the Game Theory lecture corpus and must not import that corpus's civilizational, institutional, geopolitical, or eschatological claims.

## Method

1. Identify the actors who can change the outcome, including absent decision-makers and affected parties
2. For each actor, record decision rights, dependencies, credible options, and the audience whose approval matters most
3. Map formal rules, informal norms, rewards, punishments, and constraints on exit
4. Separate stated goals from behavior-supported incentives. Treat hidden motives as hypotheses, not facts
5. Identify information asymmetries, commitments, coalitions, repeated interactions, and power differences
6. Describe the current stable pattern, if one exists, without calling every outcome an equilibrium
7. Model how each actor would probably respond to the contemplated intervention, including second-order responses
8. Look for a change to rules, information, options, sequencing, or incentives that improves the pattern without relying on goodwill alone
9. State the strongest alternative explanation for the observed behavior and the evidence that would distinguish it

Status, proximity, ostracism, and self-protection are useful candidate incentives. None is the default explanation. Prefer observable choices, constraints, and repeated behavior over stories about what an actor secretly wants.

## Completion criterion

Finish when the analysis explains the current behavior, predicts at least the first response to a proposed move, and identifies what evidence could falsify the strategic reading.
